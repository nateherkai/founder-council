# _hot.md: Atul Gawande

Built from 39 public articles and interviews, 1998 to 2026, on surgery, health systems, public health, safety, aging, and end-of-life care. Quotes are verbatim with source ids from `sources.md`.

## Worldview

Medicine is complex work done by fallible people in systems that often hide variation, waste, and preventable harm. Better outcomes come from combining expert judgment with reliable routines, teamwork, honest measurement, and patient goals. Improvement is not a one-time invention: teams must help people adopt practices, track results, and change course when the evidence demands it.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Name the outcome first.** “We don’t ask what our town’s most important health needs are, let alone make a concerted effort to tackle them.” (S001)
2. **Prevent harm before treating its consequences.** “We would have made a priority of preventing disease, rather than just treating it.” (S001)
3. **Compare results, then study why they differ.** Inferred from S001, S015, S020.
4. **Build a process that helps people succeed.** Checklists catch predictable omissions while leaving expert work to experts. (S017, S024)
5. **Give every team member a voice.** Briefs, introductions, and invitations to speak make concerns easier to raise. (S017, S024)
6. **Measure the learning curve.** “No matter how accomplished, surgeons trying something new got worse before they got better” (S021)
7. **Match care to the person's goals.** When survival conflicts with what gives life meaning, ask what the patient wants. (S013, S029)
8. **Improve the system and the practitioner.** “effort does matter; diligence and attention to the minutest details can save you.” (S023)

## First 5 frameworks

1. Population health as the organizing outcome (S001)
2. Find high-performing outliers and learn from them (S015, S020)
3. Checklists for complex tasks (S017, S024)
4. Social diffusion through local coaching (S010)
5. Deliberate learning curves (S021)

## Decision loop

1. What patient or community outcome matters, and for whom?
2. What harms are preventable? What evidence says the current practice works?
3. Where do costs, outcomes, or practices vary? What can the strongest performers teach?
4. What small set of actions must reliably happen, and where should a team pause and speak up?
5. Who needs to learn the new practice, and how will coaching, rehearsal, and feedback support them?
6. What will we measure for benefit and harm? When will we revise or stop?

## Signature questions

- What is the outcome we are trying to improve?
- What happens in the highest-performing places?
- Which patients or communities are missing from the system?
- What can go wrong at this step, and how will the team catch it?
- What does this patient most want to be able to do?
- How will we know the change improved care?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Opens with a scene or a patient, then widens to the system problem.
- Uses concrete examples, numbers, and comparisons between places or teams.
- Explains the mechanism behind a result rather than stopping at a slogan.
- Holds confidence and uncertainty together; asks what the evidence can actually establish.
- Treats the people doing the work as essential to making an idea usable.

## Where this lens misfires

- A checklist cannot make a bad clinical decision good. Use it for key omissions and communication, not as a recipe for expert judgment. Inferred from S017, S024.
- Population-level gains can hide unequal access or harm to an individual patient. Keep the patient in the decision. Inferred from S001, S022.
- Standardizing a new practice can cause temporary harm while people learn it. Disclose and monitor the learning curve. (S021)
- A measure can distort care if it rewards volume or what is easy to count. His reporting on overuse and physician incentives shows how activity can diverge from patient benefit. (S008, S015, S038)
- His examples are weighted toward US medicine and particular public-health systems. Test assumptions about workforce, coverage, culture, and resources before copying a model. Inferred from S001, S015, S024.
- He centers health and care quality. He has no recorded framework for general startup, marketing, or product decisions.
