# frameworks.md: Atul Gawande

Built from 39 public articles and interviews dated 1998 to 2026. Scores are council guidance drawn from his work, not his own scale.

## 1. Population health as the organizing outcome (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Organize care around the major health needs of a defined population, not only the complaints of people who arrive at a facility.
- **Parts:** Identify preventable causes of illness; map gaps in access and outcomes; combine prevention, primary care, and hospitals; measure changes over time.
- **When to use:** System strategy, public health, service design, and resource allocation.
- **How to score:** 5 if the intervention addresses a major measured burden and reaches the affected population; 3 if it benefits clinic users but misses broader preventable need; 1 if success is defined only by activity or spend.
- **Quote:** “We don’t ask what our town’s most important health needs are, let alone make a concerted effort to tackle them.” (S001)
- **Sources:** 4 (S001, S002, S003, S009)

## 2. Learn from outliers (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Find places with markedly different cost or outcomes, then investigate the practices and structures that explain the gap.
- **Parts:** Compare similar populations; check outcome and cost data; study care patterns; test what can be transferred.
- **When to use:** Persistent cost variation, inconsistent outcomes, or a stuck system.
- **How to score:** 5 when a peer comparison identifies a tested practice with better outcomes and no hidden selection effect; 1 when the comparison ignores population differences.
- **Quote:** “The decision is whether we are going to reward the leaders who are trying to build a new generation of Mayos and Grand Junctions.” (S015)
- **Sources:** 5 (S001, S015, S020, S032, S035)

## 3. Checklist for complex work
- **What it is:** A short prompt for a team to complete critical checks and communicate at key moments. It is a cognitive aid, not a recipe.
- **Parts:** Select pause points; identify killer omissions; keep it concise; involve the people who use it; test adoption and outcomes.
- **When to use:** High-stakes work with many steps, handoffs, or predictable omissions.
- **How to score:** 5 if the list is short, used aloud at the right moment, and tracks material errors; 1 if it is a long compliance form with no team exchange.
- **Quote:** “The degree of difficulty in any one of these steps is substantial.” (S017)
- **Sources:** 6 (S017, S024, S025, S026, S028, S030)

## 4. Team pause points (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Stop briefly before a high-risk step so the team can confirm the plan, name, role, and concerns.
- **Parts:** Introduce the team; state the objective and risks; ask for concerns; agree on next steps.
- **When to use:** Before irreversible actions and at handoffs.
- **How to score:** 5 if each role can speak and the pause catches a relevant issue; 1 if only the most senior person speaks.
- **Quote:** “The degree of difficulty in any one of these steps is substantial.” (S017)
- **Sources:** 5 (S017, S024, S025, S027, S030)

## 5. Social diffusion and coaching (unnamed)
- **What it is:** People adopt new practices through trusted relationships and support, not through information alone.
- **Parts:** Understand local barriers; show the practice; coach people in context; adapt without losing the core; measure uptake and outcomes.
- **When to use:** Rolling out care standards or any behavior change in a professional community.
- **How to score:** 5 if the plan budgets for local coaching and sustained practice; 1 if it assumes a memo or app will change behavior.
- **Quote:** “People and institutions can feel messy and anachronistic.” (S010)
- **Sources:** 4 (S010, S025, S027, S031)

## 6. High-risk, high-need care coordination (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Identify patients with concentrated, preventable use and coordinate clinical care with the social supports that affect health.
- **Parts:** Find high-use patients; understand their circumstances; assign a team; connect services; follow outcomes and costs.
- **When to use:** Emergency-heavy care, readmissions, or fragmented support.
- **How to score:** 5 when it reduces avoidable crises while improving patient stability; 1 when it only cuts access or shifts cost elsewhere.
- **Quote:** “We don’t ask what our town’s most important health needs are, let alone make a concerted effort to tackle them.” (S001)
- **Sources:** 3 (S012, S015, S029)

## 7. Deliberate learning curve
- **What it is:** New techniques create a temporary period of worse performance; structured practice can shorten the path to competence.
- **Parts:** Select a stable team; rehearse; schedule enough repetitions close together; brief and debrief; track progress; disclose risk.
- **When to use:** Introducing a new procedure, tool, or operating model.
- **How to score:** 5 if training and outcome monitoring precede scale; 1 if novelty is treated as immediate improvement.
- **Quote:** “No matter how accomplished, surgeons trying something new got worse before they got better” (S021)
- **Sources:** 4 (S021, S024, S030, S032)

## 8. Evidence before enthusiasm (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Compare expected benefit with actual evidence and downstream harms before scaling a test, procedure, or technology.
- **Parts:** Define the outcome; check study quality; compare alternatives; count false positives and downstream interventions; revisit evidence.
- **When to use:** Screening, new procedures, and treatment choices with uncertain value.
- **How to score:** 5 when benefit is measured against harms and alternatives; 1 when popularity or plausibility substitutes for outcomes.
- **Quote:** “the operation is of no help for chronic arthritis- or age-related damage.” (S008)
- **Sources:** 5 (S008, S014, S021, S035, S039)

## 9. Patient goals and end-of-life priorities (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Align treatment with the patient's priorities when disease is serious or cure is not possible.
- **Parts:** Ask what the person understands; ask what matters in daily life; discuss tradeoffs; make a plan; return to the conversation as circumstances change.
- **When to use:** Serious illness, aging, and decisions where treatment burden may exceed benefit.
- **How to score:** 5 when care advances goals the patient names; 1 when treatment continues by default without asking.
- **Quote:** “The hard question we face, then, is not how we can afford this system’s expense.” (S013)
- **Sources:** 5 (S013, S018, S027, S029, S031)

## 10. Error review and system redesign (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Treat errors as evidence about the conditions of work, while retaining individual responsibility and skill.
- **Parts:** Make errors visible; examine contributing conditions; improve the process; support or restrict unsafe practitioners; check recurrence.
- **When to use:** Complications, near misses, repeat failures, and safety review.
- **How to score:** 5 if review leads to a safer system and clear accountability; 1 if it stops at blame or excuses.
- **Quote:** “we can make dramatic improvements by going after the process, not the people.” (S023)
- **Sources:** 5 (S017, S019, S023, S038, S039)

## 11. Transparency and performance comparison (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Make meaningful differences in practice and outcomes visible so patients and professionals can learn and improve.
- **Parts:** Choose fair measures; risk-adjust; publish understandable results; investigate outliers; link findings to improvement.
- **When to use:** Provider quality, institutional accountability, and system reform.
- **How to score:** 5 when data is fair, interpretable, and connected to action; 1 when a crude ranking invites gaming.
- **Quote:** “And I think that’s where we need to have much more information.” (S024)
- **Sources:** 4 (S020, S024, S029, S031)

## 12. Incremental care when cure is unavailable (unnamed)
- **What it is:** Continue to help through relief, support, and function even when disease cannot be reversed.
- **Parts:** Name the limit of cure; clarify what matters; offer symptom relief and support; revisit the plan.
- **When to use:** Chronic illness, frailty, and terminal disease.
- **How to score:** 5 when care improves function or meaning on the patient's terms; 1 when interventions add burden without a valued benefit.
- **Quote:** “The hard question we face, then, is not how we can afford this system’s expense.” (S013)
- **Sources:** 4 (S006, S013, S018, S029)

## 13. Technology as a workflow change, not a gadget
- **What it is:** Evaluate a technology by whether it improves care, communication, or access after accounting for transition costs.
- **Parts:** Observe actual work; include users; measure learning costs and benefits; revise the tool and workflow.
- **When to use:** Electronic records, automation, and clinical technology adoption.
- **How to score:** 5 when patient benefit persists after implementation costs; 1 when deployment success means only that software was installed.
- **Quote:** “Some things were slower than they were with our old system, and some things had improved.” (S004)
- **Sources:** 3 (S004, S024, S031)

## 14. Scale the support, not just the idea
- **What it is:** Effective improvement often requires building the training, workforce, and institutions that make a practice repeatable.
- **Parts:** Identify the human work; cost it; train the roles; monitor delivery; expand based on results.
- **When to use:** Evidence-based but labor-intensive care changes.
- **How to score:** 5 when the plan funds implementation capacity; 1 when it assumes an intervention scales without people or infrastructure.
- **Quote:** “That’s exactly what has worked.” (S010)
- **Sources:** 3 (S001, S010, S015)
