# frameworks.md: Margaret MacMillan
Built from verified extraction notes and 32 public sources dated 2002–2026.

## Historical warning, not blueprint
Use earlier cases to detect hazards and generate questions, never as a template for the present. Steps: name the precedent; identify the warning; list differences; decide what evidence matters now. Use for strategic or policy choices under uncertainty. Guidance: a strong analogy identifies a mechanism that can be checked; it does not predict an identical outcome. Quote: “What I think the past can do is act like [a] warning.” (S030). Sources using it: S003, S017, S030, S031.

## Analogy with a difference check
Compare only after identifying context, actors, institutions, and goals in both cases. Use for claims that the present is “just like” a past crisis. Guidance: write at least one material difference before drawing a conclusion. Quote: “History never repeats itself completely, but there are very striking parallels.” (S010). Sources: S001, S003, S010, S017, S030.

## Structure and agency together
Read large forces and individual choices as interacting causes. Identify the social, economic, political, and ideological conditions; then ask which leaders had room to act. Use when an explanation blames either a single person or an impersonal system. Quote: “we can never abstract or take away the role of human agency and the importance of human leadership” (S030). Sources: S003, S009, S030, S031.

## Contingency and the decision point
Find the juncture where a different choice could have changed the path. Use to challenge “it had to happen” narratives. Guidance: identify a plausible alternative action and the constraint it would have faced. Quote: “I don’t think things are ever inevitable. If we think that, then we don’t do anything.” (S031). Sources: S001, S003, S030, S031.

## Escalation chain and off-ramps
Trace how an incident, commitment, fear, or public demand can narrow leaders' options until they feel unable to retreat. Use for crisis and conflict risk. Guidance: map the steps from trigger to commitment and identify where communication or a face-saving exit could interrupt them. Quote: “they got themselves into a position where they felt they couldn't back down, and that's always dangerous.” (S001). Sources: S001, S003, S009.

## History as a political tool
Ask who is invoking a past, which identity it reinforces, and which action it legitimizes. Use when policy is defended through national memory or a simplified historical story. Quote: “I think history is a very important part of identity” (S015). Sources: S014, S015, S017, S030.

## Context before judgment
Understand an actor's historical experience, interests, and self-image before interpreting present behavior. Use in cross-cultural, political, or institutional decisions. Guidance: make the actor's own account intelligible before accepting or rejecting it. Quote: “Where history can be useful is in opening our minds to other possibilities.” (S017). Sources: S003, S010, S017, S030.

## War and social organization
Treat organized violence as a social and institutional activity, not merely a breakdown of ordinary life. Ask how a society organizes people, authority, resources, and coercion, and how conflict changes those systems. Quote: “war is a highly organized activity.” (S009). Sources: S004, S009, S027.

## International order requires capable members
Assess an institution by whether powerful states support it and whether its rules can be acted on. Use for alliances, treaties, and multilateral systems. Quote: “China and the United States have to find ways they can work together” (S009). Sources: S003, S009, S021, S028.

## Peace is a negotiated settlement
Distinguish ending the fighting from building a settlement parties can live with. Include security, legitimacy, grievances, and the likely effects on excluded parties. Use after conflict or major institutional change. Quote: “Things can go wrong, and we can have accidents in human affairs” (S003). Sources: S003, S010, S027.

## Interconnected crisis map
Map the channels through which one crisis affects another: weapons, resources, alliances, information, trade, and public opinion. Use where multiple conflicts or systems are linked. Quote: “The war in the Middle East is connected to the war in Ukraine.” (S001). Sources: S001, S003, S009.

## Leaders are products of their worlds
Examine personality without detaching it from formative experience, political culture, and institutional setting. Use when a leader's style appears decisive. Quote: “They are products of the forces of their own times; they come out of certain worlds; they think in certain ways.” (S003). Sources: S003, S009, S030.

## Social effects of conflict
Track who bears costs and how wartime demands can alter participation, taxation, rights, and social status. Use to understand how institutions change after crises. Quote: “war is a highly organized activity.” (S009). Sources: S004, S009, S027.

## Test the narrative against evidence
Separate a vivid story from what the record supports. Ask what is missing, whose evidence is absent, and whether hindsight has made the outcome seem inevitable. Use in public arguments and organizational retrospectives. Quote: “you can pretty much find anything you want.” (S030). Sources: S006, S014, S015, S030.
