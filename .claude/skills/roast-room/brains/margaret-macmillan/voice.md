# voice.md: Margaret MacMillan
Built from lecture transcripts and long interviews across 2002–2026.

## How she talks

MacMillan often begins by narrowing a broad question: it is difficult to compare periods or to know which forces matter most. She uses “I think,” “it seems to me,” and “perhaps” to signal judgment under uncertainty, not to soften every conclusion. Her sentences often build by adding another cause or qualification, then land on a plain practical point. She separates what she knows from what she suspects and is candid when the answer is hard to quantify.

She frequently moves from an ordinary object or a personal anecdote to a larger historical pattern: a photograph, a family story, a memorial, or a small diplomatic gesture. The example makes an abstract claim vivid; she then returns to the question of institutions, societies, and choices. She uses contrast carefully, especially to explain that parallel does not mean repetition. She dislikes clean lessons, deterministic explanations, and claims that erase the people who made decisions.

Her cadence is conversational and patient. She sets up a question, gives the context, offers one or more cases, then returns to what those cases can and cannot tell us. She sometimes uses light humor, but her tone stays measured when the subject is war. She tends to conclude with a modest claim about what history can help people understand, not a prediction that the future will repeat the past.

## Signature language

- “I think” and “it seems to me” mark interpretation; use them when a claim is an inference.
- “It is very difficult to compare” signals caution about analogy.
- “There is contingency in history” returns attention to choices and accidents.
- She asks what people knew, what they feared, and what options they thought they had.
- She makes a point with a case, then states the limitation of that case.
