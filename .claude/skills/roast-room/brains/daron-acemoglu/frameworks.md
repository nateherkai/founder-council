# Frameworks: Daron Acemoglu

Built from 30 public sources from 2002 to 2026. Most underlying papers are represented by NBER records and abstracts; apply their models as lenses, not as a substitute for the full papers.

## 1. Task-based model of technological change
Production is a collection of tasks assigned to people with different skills or to capital. Technology can augment existing worker tasks, automate tasks, deepen automation, or create new tasks. These changes have different effects on labor demand, wages, labor share, and output. Use it to map what work changes before predicting the effect of a technology. Scoring guidance: count the tasks affected, who performs them now, who can perform them after the change, and the expected demand for each group. Quote: "Production in each industry requires the completion of various tasks that can be assigned to workers with different skills or to capital." (S025). Found in S014, S021-S025.

## 2. Displacement and reinstatement
Automation shifts work from labor to capital and displaces workers from tasks where they had an advantage. New tasks can reinstate labor by expanding the work people do. Use it to assess an automation proposal. Scoring guidance: score displacement and new task creation separately; do not count cost savings as worker gains. Quote: "The introduction of new tasks changes the task content of production in favor of labor because of a reinstatement effect" (S014). Found in S014, S021-S025.

## 3. Productivity effect versus displacement effect
Automation can lower costs and raise demand for non-automated labor, while also removing labor tasks. These countervailing effects do not guarantee workers share in productivity gains. Use it to ask which jobs expand and who captures the savings. Scoring guidance: estimate saved costs, induced demand, wage changes, and labor share independently. Quote: "The productivity effect is complemented by additional capital accumulation and the deepening of automation" (S023). Found in S021-S024.

## 4. The new-task test
Productivity tools that let people perform new, higher-value work can create demand for human skill; tools that only make existing tasks cheaper can displace workers or increase inequality. Use it in product and AI adoption choices. Scoring guidance: name the new human task, its beneficiary, and how demand for it will arise. Quote: "What new tasks do is that they expand what they do." (S002). Found in S002, S004, S014, S023-S025.

## 5. Institutional causes of development
Institutions shape incentives and constraints for economic actors and thereby affect outcomes. They are social choices made amid conflicts among groups with different interests. Use it when a decision depends on rule enforcement, property rights, fair access, or political stability. Scoring guidance: trace who can make or change the rules and who gains from each arrangement. Quote: "Economic institutions determine the incentives of and the constraints on economic actors, and shape economic outcomes." (S015). Found in S001, S005, S007, S015-S020.

## 6. Inclusive and extractive institutions
Inclusive institutions broaden economic and political participation and protect rights. Extractive institutions concentrate power and resources. These are patterns, not simple labels for a country or firm. Use it to test whether a business or policy creates broad opportunity or privileges a narrow group. Scoring guidance: identify who can participate, challenge decisions, and keep returns. Quote: "The variation in this figure is very much caused by the fact that extractive institutions are everywhere." (S001). Found in S001, S005-S007, S015.

## 7. De jure and de facto power
Formal rules allocate de jure power, while resources and the capacity to organize, lobby, protest, or subvert determine de facto power. Their interaction explains both institutional persistence and change. Use it to assess governance, stakeholder leverage, and regulatory capture. Scoring guidance: map formal authority and practical influence separately. Quote: "The interplay of de facto and de jure power is a source of persistence and change." (S001). Found in S001, S017, S020, S030.

## 8. Dynamic institutional persistence
Political rules and resource distributions change slowly; economic arrangements feed back into both. Today's winners may seek to preserve or strengthen their future power. Use it when a short-term policy or investment may reshape future bargaining power. Scoring guidance: follow the likely feedback over several rounds, not just the first-order effect. Quote: "These variables themselves change over time because prevailing economic institutions affect the distribution of resources" (S015). Found in S001, S015, S017, S030.

## 9. Political commitment and social conflict
Groups with power may choose inefficient policies when promises cannot be credibly enforced and gains are contested. Use it to assess contracts with governments, partnerships, regulatory commitments, and transitions that create winners and losers. Scoring guidance: ask who can renege, who can enforce, and whether a losing group can block implementation. Quote: "At the center of the theoretical case are the commitment problems inherent in politics" (S020). Found in S017, S019-S020, S030.

## 10. Historical comparison and quasi-natural experiments
Historical variation can help distinguish institutional causes from geography, culture, or other correlated factors. Korea's division and colonial settlement patterns are examples in the corpus. Use it when people offer an attractive but untested causal story. Scoring guidance: name the counterfactual, the source of variation, and plausible confounders. Quote: "You need something more than just a correlation" (S001). Found in S001, S005, S007, S015.

## 11. Democracy as an economic input
Democracy may support growth through investment, education, reform, public goods, and lower social unrest. Acemoglu and coauthors report a positive long-run GDP effect in their paper. Use it when assessing political accountability as an economic institution. Scoring guidance: specify which channel is expected and what measurable result would support it. Quote: "Our results suggest that democracy increases future GDP by encouraging investment, increasing schooling, inducing economic reforms, improving public good provision, and reducing social unrest." (S018). Found in S018, S026, S030.

## 12. AI macroeconomic accounting
Estimate macro effects from the fraction of tasks affected and task-level cost or productivity changes, then check whether early evidence generalizes to harder tasks. The 2024 paper estimates modest aggregate productivity gains under its assumptions and discusses distribution separately. Use it for AI business cases. Scoring guidance: identify exposure, achievable performance gains, adoption costs, and whether tasks have objective feedback. Quote: "These macroeconomic effects appear nontrivial but modest" (S024). Found in S004, S010, S024.

## 13. Social value of new tasks
Not every new task is socially beneficial. Online manipulation is cited as an example of a task with negative social value. Use it when increased revenue or engagement could come from harmful activity. Scoring guidance: score who benefits and who bears the social cost, not only productivity or revenue. Quote: "some of the new tasks created by AI may have negative social value" (S024). Found in S024.

## 14. Technology direction and machine usefulness
Society can steer innovation toward worker substitution or toward tools that complement human capabilities. Use it to distinguish a technology's capability from the business choice about how to deploy it. Scoring guidance: ask whether the tool makes people more capable, creates demand for human expertise, and broadens gains. Quote: "we want them to be complements to humans." (S010). Found in S001-S004, S010, S023-S025.

## 15. State capacity and social constraints
Effective public authority and organized society constrain one another. Weak states may fail to provide security or services; unconstrained states can dominate society. Use it for institutional or market-entry decisions in settings with different governance capacities. Scoring guidance: check both the ability to implement rules and the ability of people to hold power accountable. Quote: "There are thousands of different versions of democracy." (S007). Found in S007, S030.
