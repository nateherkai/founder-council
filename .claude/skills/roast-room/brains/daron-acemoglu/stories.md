# Stories: Daron Acemoglu

Built from historical examples reused in interviews, lectures, and the cited research summaries.

1. **North and South Korea:** A shared peninsula divided into contrasting institutional systems helps illustrate how institutions can shape economic outcomes (S015).
2. **Colonial settlement patterns:** European colonialism produced different institutional arrangements across territories; the historical shock offers variation for causal research, but does not dictate present outcomes (S001, S015).
3. **Botswana's diamonds:** Seretse Khama's government used legislation and state institutions to secure mineral rights for Botswana, illustrating how agency and lawful institutions can change the distribution of resource gains (S001, S007).
4. **The British Industrial Revolution:** Productivity and output grew before ordinary workers saw broad improvements. Worker organization, education, political voice, and institutions helped determine how gains were shared (S004, S007).
5. **The Luddites:** Opposition to machinery also reflected real concerns about wages, job loss, and worker voice. Technology can create winners and losers before its longer-run effects are clear (S004, S010).
6. **Ford's factories and electrification:** New technology and mass production created demand for new skills and jobs, an example of technology reinstating labor through new tasks (S002, S004).
7. **The postwar U.S. boom:** GDP and wages grew together for a period, showing shared prosperity can happen, not that it follows automatically from growth (S004).
8. **China's growth and governance:** Rapid growth and industrial success coexist with low-wage labor, restricted voice, and capital misallocation. The case cautions against treating growth rate as the whole welfare story (S007).
9. **U.S. financial crisis and the ownership society:** Acemoglu disputes a simple inequality-to-housing-regulation explanation and instead traces political incentives and institutional change as parts of the crisis story (S005).
10. **The stocking frame and William Lee:** Queen Elizabeth's refusal to patent knitting machinery reflected fears of unemployment and political instability, showing how incumbent power can shape the path of invention (S010).
11. **The iPhone and spillovers:** Successful products can create substantial value beyond a firm's direct returns, prompting the question of how innovation affects consumers and complementary work (S005).
12. **Botswana versus Mauritius:** Botswana's institutional trajectory improved through political choices, while Acemoglu says Mauritius's later governance deterioration should not be treated as an act of God (S001).
