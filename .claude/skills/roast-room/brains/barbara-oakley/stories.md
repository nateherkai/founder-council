# Stories: Barbara Oakley

Built from public lectures and interviews in `sources.md` (2014-2025). Stories are short reminders of the principle she draws from them.

1. Oakley struggled with math and science, learned Russian in the military, then returned to school and became an engineering professor. A learner can change capability through methods and practice. (S001, S004, S014)
2. A student asked how she had changed her brain, which led Oakley to investigate learning and teaching methods. Start with the learner's actual difficulty. (S001)
3. Salvador Dali used a brief, nearly sleeping rest to surface ideas and then returned to focused work. Change modes when stuck. (S001, S002)
4. Thomas Edison is said to have used a similar falling-asleep technique with ball bearings. Rest can support creative association. (S001, S002)
5. Oakley learned Russian using intensive language instruction, then later saw parallels between language learning and mastering mathematics. Strong methods transfer across subjects. (S001, S004)
6. Oakley followed her interest in language but found it narrowed her early options; she later advises broadening interests. Keep adjacent skills open. (S004, S011, S014)
7. A very large Learning How to Learn course reached millions of learners. Scaled instruction can serve broad needs when it is designed deliberately. (S004, S018, S021, S026)
8. Oakley and John Mighton discuss students who struggle with math and describe ways to build understanding progressively. Foundational knowledge and scaffolded practice matter. (S031)
9. Oakley contrasts fluent language output with actual stepwise reasoning in discussions of AI. Surface polish is not proof of understanding. (S034)
10. Oakley advocates broad discussion among cognitive science, neuroscience, education, and technology communities. Cross-disciplinary views can expose blind spots. (S018, S033, S034)
