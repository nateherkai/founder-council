# Frameworks: Richard H. Thaler

Built from the quick-depth interview and event corpus listed in sources.md. Most entries come from attributed remarks or the speaker's own examples.

## 1. Choice architecture (S001, S013, S014)

The environment in which people make choices. Anyone who sets the order, defaults, layout, wording, or steps is shaping that environment, whether deliberately or not. Use it when people repeatedly make a choice in a designed setting. Review the path from the user's point of view and check where a small change may predictably alter behavior. The source material gives no general score thresholds. Quote: “People should understand that they are the recipients of choice architecture in almost every aspect of their life.” (S012)

## 2. Nudge criteria (S014, S017)

A feature of the choice environment influences behavior while people retain their options. Use a nudge when the goal is to help people act on a preference they already have, and where the intervention is easy to avoid. The distinction matters: a nudge leaves choice; a mandate or price change does not. Judge it by observed behavior, not the designer's intentions. Quote: “The idea of a nudge is that you can point somebody in a helpful direction without forcing them to do anything.” (S005)

## 3. Make it easy (S014)

Reduce unnecessary steps between intention and action. The retirement plan example combines automatic enrollment, automatic escalation, and a default investment fund so people do not need to complete forms or design the whole plan. Use this lens when a reasonable action is delayed or abandoned. First simplify; if that does not work, test another intervention. No universal effect size is supported by this corpus. Quote: “Nudges typically work by making something easy, like automatically signing you up for the retirement plan.” (S014)

## 4. Sludge audit (S009, S012, S013, S014, S016)

Look for friction that makes it hard to switch, cancel, claim, or complete an action. Compare entry and exit steps, required time, confusing information, and channel restrictions. The London newspaper cancellation story shows how a cheap trial can be structured to make exit impractical. Ask whether the burden is necessary, accidental, or designed to discourage action. Measure the user cost before calling it efficient. Quote: “If you make things harder, I call that sludge.” (S016)

## 5. Supposedly irrelevant factors (S017)

Test details that a standard model treats as immaterial, including wording and defaults. Thaler's point is empirical: apparently small elements may change behavior. Use a controlled comparison when feasible. The corpus does not supply a universal threshold for meaningful effect. Quote: “I coined the phrase supposedly irrelevant factors for the kinds of things that economists are sure don't matter, like the way a letter is worded or what the default option is.” (S017)

## 6. Sunk-cost check (S017)

For the next decision, compare future benefits with future costs. Do not stay at an unpleasant concert only because the ticket was expensive. Use this when people say they must continue to make past spending worthwhile. The model does not imply that past investments never matter; it says the irrecoverable payment is not itself a reason to choose the same future action. Quote: “You should just decide whether the music is worth the annoyance of the rain.” (S017)

## 7. Save More Tomorrow and automatic escalation (S001, S007, S018)

Let workers commit in advance to saving more from future pay increases. Pair enrollment with an automatic increase and a clear default. The design reduces the immediate felt cost and makes follow-through easier. The sources report strong results in the original program, but provide little detail on current population fit or long-term outcomes, so treat those figures as historical, not as a forecast for a new plan. Quote: “making the raises automatic and getting people signed up.” (S012)

## 8. Human versus idealized agent (S008, S017)

Separate a model of optimal choice from a model that predicts actual behavior. If observed behavior repeatedly departs from the assumed rational model, test an account that includes real limits and motives. Use evidence rather than defending assumptions by adding exceptions. This is a broad research lens, not a ready-made prediction for every customer. Quote: “The idea that markets work perfectly is no longer tenable.” (S017)

## 9. Defaults are not proof of considered consent (S012, S013)

An opt-out choice can raise participation, but failure to opt out may reflect inattention. Ask whether the user saw and understood the default, whether an easy alternative exists, and what happens after the decision. Organ donation is especially sensitive because registration, family preferences, and actual transplant procedures are distinct. Do not infer a considered preference from silence alone. Quote: “If he failed to fill out some form, how confident would you be that that omission was based on careful thought? Right? Zero.” (S012)

## 10. Combine behavioral and structural tools (S001, S002, S012, S013)

Nudges can help people respond to incentives, but cannot replace sound incentives or necessary regulation. Thaler's climate examples pair easier choices with carbon pricing; his gun-safety discussion allows both choice design and restrictions. Identify the scale of the failure before choosing the tool. No single intervention type is sufficient in every case. Quote: “You’ve got to get the prices right. And then nudging can help, and every 1 percent helps.” (S012)
